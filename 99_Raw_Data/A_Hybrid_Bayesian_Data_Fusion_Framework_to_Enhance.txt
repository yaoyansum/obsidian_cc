A Hybrid Bayesian Data Fusion Framework to Enhance Watershed Nitrogen Modeling by Integrating In Situ Observations With Spatial Inversion Data
Shunping Zhou1,2 , Pan Yang1,2 , Yuheng Gan1,2, and Qian Tan1,2
1Guangdong Basic Research Center of Excellence for Ecological Security and Green Development, School of Ecology, Environment and Ocean, Guangdong University of Technology, Guangzhou, China, 2Guangdong Provincial Key Laboratory of Water Quality Improvement and Ecological Restoration for Watersheds, School of Ecology, Environment and Ocean, Guangdong University of Technology, Guangzhou, China
Abstract Process-based watershed models require robust, spatially distributed data for accurate total
nitrogen (TN) simulation. However, traditional calibrations are often constrained by sparse in situ monitoring networks, resulting in substantial estimation errors and biases, particularly in ungauged sub-basins. While newly available spatial inversion data offer high-resolution observations of TN, their complex, heterogeneous, and non-stationary error structures pose challenges for rigorous integration into process-based models. This study presents a Hybrid Inversion-Observation Bayesian Data Fusion (HIOBF) framework to address this issue. The framework incorporates a geography-driven spatial extrapolation model to characterize inversion data errors in unmonitored areas, systematically merging high-resolution spatial inversion data with conventional point-scale measurements. We implemented and tested this framework using the Soil and Water Assessment Tool model in the Dongjiang River Basin, China. Results from independent validation demonstrated that by synergizing the spatial coverage of inversion data with the temporal precision of gauge measurements, the HIOBF framework significantly reduced parametric uncertainty and improved the Nash-Sutcliffe Efficiency by an average margin of 55% compared to the traditional gauge measurement-only baseline. This work provides a transferable methodology, offering a robust approach to enhance simulation accuracy and provide defensible uncertainty estimates for water resource management in data-scarce contexts.
1. Introduction
Process-based watershed modeling has become increasingly critical under accelerating global environmental change and intensified agricultural nutrient loading. Tools such as Soil and Water Assessment Tool (SWAT) (Gassman et al., 2007), Hydrological Simulation Program-FORTRAN (Johanson et al., 1980) and Annualized Agricultural Non-Point Source Pollution (AnnAGNPS) model (Bingner et al., 2003) have been widely adopted for their ability to represent hydrological and biogeochemical processes across scales (Fu et al., 2019; Keller et al., 2023; Valeh et al., 2021; Wellen et al., 2015). Their effective application hinges on the ability to generate credible, spatially explicit predictions across all internal sub-basins and river reaches (Abbaspour et al., 2007; Chen et al., 2024)--a prerequisite for targeting Best Management Practices (BMPs) (Johnson & Sample, 2017; Lemke et al., 2011), supporting early warning systems (Liu et al., 2024; Qian et al., 2024), and advancing understandings of watershed biogeochemistry (Kaushal et al., 2018; Yang et al., 2024).
Despite their process-based rigor and widespread adoption (Dile et al., 2020), models such as SWAT are generally calibrated using limited in situ measurements of streamflow and nutrient concentrations at only a few monitoring stations (Malagó et al., 2017; Moazenzadeh & Izady, 2022; Rahman et al., 2020). While this outlet-centered calibration may yield acceptable performance in well-instrumented sub-basins, it fails to constrain sub-basins and river reaches where observations are scarce or absent (Paul et al., 2021; Yu et al., 2025). This creates a structurally ill-posed problem, where estimating a large set of internal parameters from a limited set of outlet observations leads to equifinality--multiple parameter sets yielding similar outputs (Beven, 1989, 2006). Extrapolating parameters from gauged to ungauged sub-basins overlooks local heterogeneity in geomorphology, land use, and pollution sources, increasing parameter uncertainty (Qi et al., 2020), reducing predictive accuracy, and introducing spatial inconsistency (Guo et al., 2005; Panchanathan et al., 2023).

To alleviate the spatial constraints of conventional monitoring, recent studies have explored alternative ground- based data sources--notably crowdsourced measurements and low-cost sensors--that enable denser, though lower-quality, monitoring networks (Akhie & Joksimovic, 2023; Avellaneda et al., 2020; Liu et al., 2020; Mazzoleni et al., 2018; Poupry et al., 2023; Wongchuig et al., 2024; Yang & Ng, 2019). These opportunistic data stream have shown promise in better constraining dynamic model processes. For example, assimilating crowdsourced observations, despite their asynchronous and uncertain nature, with traditional, static sensor networks has improved flood forecast performance by 10%­16% (Songchon et al., 2023). Similarly, assimilating sub-hourly in situ electrical conductivity sensors in SWAT has reduced the modeling uncertainty and management cost by 70% (Han et al., 2023). However, while these strategies successfully increase the number of observation points, they introduce challenges related to data quality control, unknown measurement errors, and systematic biases. More importantly, their long-term reliability is hindered by difficulties in sustaining public participation and sensor maintenance (Zheng et al., 2018). There remains a critical need for more reliable alternative water quality data sources to complement sparse gauge networks in watershed modeling.
In recent years, the scientific community has increasingly turned to spatially continuous data products. Among these, high-frequency spatial inversion data derived from big earth data technologies represents a reliable and powerful solution, providing the synoptic coverage, that is, impossible to achieve with point-scale measurements (Frederiksen et al., 2025; Isles, 2024; Rahat et al., 2023). The value of this approach is well-established in water quantity modeling, where the assimilation of analogous spatial data for variables such as water surface elevation and flood extent has markedly improved large-scale hydrologic and hydrodynamic forecasting (Dasgupta et al., 2021; Wongchuig et al., 2024; Wongchuig-Correa et al., 2020). Such multi-source data fusion is typically performed via the Bayesian calibration (BC) framework (T. J. Smith & Marshall, 2008; Vrugt et al., 2009; Zheng & Han, 2015). By applying Bayes' rule, BC combines prior parameter knowledge with a likelihood function that quantifies the probability of observing the data given a particular parameter set (Vrugt et al., 2009). This likelihood is established by explicitly modeling the residual between model outputs and observations (Evin et al., 2013; Schoups & Vrugt, 2010), and the resulting posterior parameter distribution is typically explored using simulation algorithms such as Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) (Gelman et al., 1995). Critically, BC allows for the incorporation of heterogeneous data types with distinct error structures, making it well-suited for assimilating both sparse, high-quality in situ data and spatially extensive, error-prone inversion products (Han & Zheng, 2016, 2018; Nascimento et al., 2023; Xie et al., 2024).
The integration of such inversion data in water quality modeling is comparatively underdeveloped (Cho et al., 2020), despite recent advancements in water quality (e.g., total nitrogen) inversion that have demonstrated strong regional accuracy (Virro et al., 2022; Zhang et al., 2024). The primary barrier to their use is not a lack of potential, but rather the methodological difficulty of their assimilation (Ribeiro et al., 2022; Shi et al., 2022; Song et al., 2022), specifically the formulation of likelihood function in the BC framework for spatial inversion water quality data at ungauged subwatersheds.
The formulation of spatial inversion water quality data errors and associated likelihood functions at ungauged subwatersheds is not straightforward. Traditionally, assuming a consistent error structure over space and time, the error structure of TN observation can be determined a priori based on historical in situ data (He et al., 2025; Wei et al., 2024). But such assumption rarely stands for spatial inversion products as their errors are complex, spatially non-stationary, and temporally autocorrelated (Rahat et al., 2023). The difficulty thus lies in a reliable "guess" of the error structure at ungauged locations. To the best of the authors' knowledge, this remains a key methodological gap, limiting the full potential of high-coverage inversion data in watershed water quality modeling.
To address the challenge of data fusion in watershed modeling, this study introduces a novel Hybrid Inversion­ Observation Bayesian Data Fusion (HIOBF) framework, specifically designed to integrate recently developed TN inversion data (https://engine-aiearth.aliyun.com/#/space/detail /kMie305012) into the SWAT model. Crucially, this study addresses the above barrier of spatial inversion data error formulation by introducing a geography- driven spatial extrapolation model. This approach is grounded in the assumption that the error structure of spatial inversion data can be reliably inferred from key sub-basin characteristics--such as catchment area, mean annual discharge, and spatial proximity--using established machine learning techniques. The geography-driven spatial extrapolation model act as a major modification of the traditional BC framework, which allows the reliable formulation of the likelihood function of spatial inversion data at ungauged sub-basins. We implement and test this framework in the Dongjiang River Basin (DJB), China, through three distinct fusion scenarios: (a) purely in

2. Methodology
2.1. Study Framework
Our methodological workflow (Figure 1) employs a robust Bayesian framework to enhance the simulation of streamflow and TN within the DJB. The process follows a structured, sequential approach (Hsu et al., 2009; Seis et al., 2024; Zhang & Arhonditsis, 2009). First, a Sobol global sensitivity analysis identifies the most influential SWAT parameters for both streamflow and TN simulations. Next, a deterministic calibration of the flow-related parameters is conducted using the Differential Evolution algorithm. Finally, the TN-related parameters are calibrated via a stochastic Bayesian calibration framework.
The framework's key innovation--the strategic integration of diverse data sources--is implemented in the third step, following the calibration of flow-related parameters. This step fuses daily in situ TN observations (Z) with high-frequency spatial inversion data (U), the latter offering critical spatial coverage. To make the inversion data suitable for Bayesian analysis, two distinct processing branches are employed. For gauged sub-basins, inversion data are combined with in situ observations to inform a Gaussian error model. For the more challenging ungauged sub-basins, a geography-driven spatial extrapolation method is employed to establish a robust relationship between the inversion data and their in situ equivalents. For both data streams, error structures are explicitly characterized, incorporating heteroscedasticity and correcting for temporal autocorrelation, to formulate a robust and integrative likelihood function.
With flow parameters fixed and the likelihood function defined, stochastic Bayesian calibration is conducted for the TN-related parameters. Posterior sampling is conducted using an efficient MCMC sampler DREAMzs (Laloy & Vrugt, 2012). To assess the contribution of each data source, three distinct data fusion scenarios are evaluated: Pure Observation Bayesian Data Fusion (POBF), PIBF, and the proposed Hybrid Inversion-Observation Bayesian Fusion (HIOBF). Model outputs from each scenario are rigorously validated against an independent multi-station data set to demonstrate the effectiveness of the proposed hybrid approach.

This framework is grounded in foundational Bayesian principles and incorporates novel components tailored to the complex error structures of high-frequency spatial inversion data. 2.4. Data Sources
Daily streamflow data were collected from two key hydrological monitoring stations within the DJB: Heyuan and Boluo. The period 2014­2017 was used for calibration, and 2019­2022 for validation. In situ total nitrogen (TN) concentration measurements from six cross-sectional monitoring stations during 2021­2022 were used for water quality calibration and validation. Specifically, calibration was performed using TN data from Jiulongtan (sub- basin 17), Shilongnanhe (sub-basin 21) and Dadun (sub-basin 27). Validation utilized data from three independent stations: Longchuan City Railway Bridge (Longchuan, sub-basin 11), Huizhou Ruhu (Ruhu, sub-basin 19), and Boluo Chengxia Xinjiao (Boluo (Xinjiao), sub-basin 24). It is noted that the TN validation station Longchuan and the Heyuan hydrological station (approximately 84.2 km apart) both locate in sub-basin 11, while the Boluo (Xinjiao) TN station and the Boluo hydrological station (approximately 7.1 km apart) both locate in sub-basin 24. The distances between water quality and hydrological stations in sub-basins 11 and 24 ensure sufficient spatial independence for TN validation purposes. Crucially, the Ruhu station serves as a fully independent TN validation site, entirely unaffected by data used in the prior hydrological or water quality calibration steps. All basic data required for the SWAT model setup, including Digital Elevation Models, land use maps, soil maps, climate data, and point source pollution data, are detailed in Table S1 of the Supporting Information S1.

The spatial TN inversion data used in this study is from a proprietary Pearl River Basin daily TN concentration data set previously developed by our research group and publicly available on the AI-Earth platform (https://engi ne-aiearth.aliyun.com/#/space/detail/kMie305012). This high-precision data set, generated using spatial- geographical big data technology based on 88 TN monitoring stations across the Pearl River Basin, provides daily, spatially continuous 5-km resolution coverage from 2015 to 2024. The 6 water quality monitoring stations used in this study (3 calibration and 3 validation stations) in Figure 2 were not included in the training of the TN inversion data. Figure S1 in the Supporting Information S1 illustrates the daily gridded TN data for the Pearl River Basin during the study period (2021­2022) extracted from the AI-Earth platform, highlighting its spatial coverage and resolution. It is important to note that the inversion data set relies in part on VIIRS-DNB nighttime-light observations, which are severely degraded by cloud cover and atmospheric aerosols. By applying stringent cloud- masking and aerosol-screening filters, we retained only those acquisition dates with reliable VIIRS-DNB signals --resulting in approximately 200 valid days in 2021 and 2022. Despite this temporal intermittency, a comparison of its daily estimation at the 6 water quality stations against our collected in situ observation data suggests a high degree of agreement in spatial TN distribution: the R2 of inversion and in situ observation data exceeds 0.8 for over 90% of the valid days, while RMSE remains below 0.4 for nearly 80% of valid days in both 2021 and 2022 (Figure S2 of the Supporting Information S1).
To integrate this spatially continuous data with the point-based SWAT model outputs, a hydrologically consistent framework was employed. First, to ensure data quality, the raw time series for each inversion data pixel was screened, and any data points falling outside of 1.5 times the interquartile range from the long-term median were removed as statistical outliers (Dash et al., 2023; Hoaglin et al., 1986). For gauged sub-basins, the inversion data is identified as the screened inversion data corresponding to the location of the monitoring station. For ungauged sub-basins, a nearest-neighbor assignment method was adopted: selecting the screened data point closest to the SWAT's sub-basin outlet. The relationship between this assigned inversion data and the in situ equivalent values was then established using the spatial extrapolation model (detailed in Section 2.2.3).
2.5. Sensitivity Analysis for Parameters to Be Calibrated
To identify the most influential parameters for subsequent calibration, we employed the Sobol global sensitivity analysis to quantify the contribution of SWAT model parameters to the uncertainty in both streamflow and TN simulations (Ballester-Ripoll et al., 2019; Sobol, 2001). The sensitivity analysis was conducted for 28 parameters that are considered influential for streamflow and total nitrogen simulation performance (Table S2 in Supporting Information S1). The detailed methodology, equations, and the specific sampling strategy used for the variance decomposition framework are provided in Text S3 of the Supporting Information S1.
2.6. Flow-Related Parameter Calibration
Following the identification of critical parameters through sensitivity analysis, an initial deterministic calibration was performed for the identified streamflow-related parameters. We employed a rigorous split-sample test strategy covering the period from 2011 to 2022. A 3-year warm-up period (2011­2013) was used to initialize and stabilize the model states. Daily streamflow observations from the Heyuan and Boluo stations for 2014­2017 served as the calibration period. Model validation was then conducted for the subsequent 2019­2022 period. Note that daily streamflow data for the year 2018 were unavailable due to monitoring gaps and were therefore excluded from the analysis.
For the calibration, the Differential Evolution (DE) algorithm (Storn & Price, 1997) was employed via the DEoptim package in R. Key hyperparameters for the algorithm were selected to ensure a thorough search of the parameter space for the 16 calibrated parameters: the population size (NP) was set to 160, the crossover probability (CR) was 0.9, and the differential weighting factor (F) was 0.8. The optimization was run for a maximum of 500 generations (itermax), resulting in 80,000 total model simulations to explore the parameter space and converge toward an optimal solution. Model performance during this process was quantitatively assessed using a suite of statistical metrics, including the Nash-Sutcliffe Efficiency (NSE), the coefficient of determination (R2), and the percent bias (PBIAS), with the objective function set to maximize the NSE.

2.7. Bayesian Data Fusion Scenarios for Water Quality-Related Parameters
To rigorously evaluate the performance of the proposed Hybrid Inversion-Observation Bayesian Data Fusion (HIOBF) method, particularly its efficacy in data-scarce regions, this study designed three distinct data fusion scenarios for comparative validation. Due to the high computational demands of Bayesian fusion, the TN simulation window was set to 2018­2022. This includes a requisite 3-year burn-in period (2018­2020) to stabilize nutrient pools, followed by a calibration period of 2021­2022. In this phase, TN parameters were calibrated while fixing the streamflow parameters inherited from the long-term hydrological model (Section 2.6). The Bayesian calibration for TN load parameters within each fusion scenario was conducted by fixing the previously calibrated streamflow parameters. The MCMC sampling was performed using the BayesianTools package in R, with DREAMzs employed for posterior sampling. Each calibration run generated up to 500,000 samples, and Markov chain convergence was confirmed using the Gelman-Rubin diagnostic (Gelman & Rubin, 1992). The initial 30% of samples from each chain were discarded as a burn-in period, with the last 50% of all points from the Markov chains used for subsequent analysis. The R code for the likelihood functions of in situ observation data (Equation 4) and spatial inversion data (Equation 11) is provided in Text S1 of Supporting Information S1; specific MCMC parameter settings are detailed in Text S2 of Supporting Information S1. The overall model calibration performance is presented in Section 3.4. These scenarios are structured to isolate and quantify the incremental value of integrating different data sources:
1. Pure Observation Bayesian Data Fusion (POBF): This baseline scenario represents a traditional approach, where model parameters are calibrated solely using in situ TN observations from a subset of gauged sub-basins (specifically, sub-basins 17, 21, and 27). This setup provides a benchmark against which the benefits of incorporating inversion data can be assessed.
2. Pure Inversion Bayesian Data Fusion (PIBF): This scenario explores the potential of inversion data as a standalone input for model calibration. It utilizes TN concentrations from a larger set of sub-basins (9, 12, 13, 22, 23, and 26), demonstrating the utility of spatial inversion data in the absence of direct ground observations.
3. Hybrid Inversion-Observation Bayesian Data Fusion (HIOBF): This is the core proposed method, designed to leverage the complementary strengths of both in situ observations and inversion data. It integrates data from all available gauged and inversion-covered sub-basins (9, 12, 13, 17, 21, 22, 23, 26, and 27). Crucially, data fusion in this scenario is spatially stratified: high-quality in situ observations are prioritized at gauged locations (using L(Zreal|)), while inversion data are fused exclusively at ungauged locations (using L(U|)).
It is noted that this experimental design involves asymmetric sample sizes (3 in situ sites vs. 6 inversion sites). This structure was intentionally chosen to reflect the realistic disparity between typically sparse monitoring networks and spatially continuous earth observation data. To isolate the effect of data quantity versus information type, a comprehensive scalability analysis with varying sample sizes is provided in Section 4.1.
All three fusion scenarios are consistently validated against an independent set of three sub-basins (11, 19, and 24) to ensure a fair and robust comparison of their predictive capabilities across different data availability conditions. This comparative experimental design allows for a clear quantification of the added value of multi-source fusion in addressing the critical challenge of data scarcity in watershed modeling.

3.4. Multi-Source Data Fusion Performance
With the error structures corrected and the spatial relationships established, this subsection assesses how the integration of different data sources impacts both the model's parametric uncertainty and its predictive accuracy in independent validation sub-basins.

4. Discussions
4.1. Scalability and Robustness of Data Fusion Benefits
While the main analysis in Section 3.4.2 demonstrated the power of the fully configured HIOBF scenario (3 in situ + 6 inversion sites), supplementary experiments were conducted to assess how performance scales when augmenting a sparse in situ network with spatial inversion data (detailed in the SI, Table S5 in Supporting Information S1). The average performance across the independent validation sites for each experiment is summarized in Figure 8.
The results reveal a clear and consistent trend: at every level of in situ data availability--whether using 1, 2, or 3 gauges--the HIOBF approach substantially outperform the traditional observation-only (POBF) baseline. For instance, augmenting a single in situ site with inversion data increases the average NSE by approximately 81% (from 0.16 to 0.29). As more in situ "anchor" sites are added, performance continues to climb non-linearly. This consistent superiority confirms that the performance gain of HIOBF stems from the complementarity of information types (the broad spatial coverage of inversion data vs. the temporal precision of in situ gauges) rather than merely the volume of data points assimilated. The consistency of this improvement across individual validation sites is detailed in Table S6 of Supporting Information S1 and visualized in the dumbbell plots in Figure S9 of the Supporting Information S1. This effectively addresses the concern that improvements might be artifacts of unequal sample sizes, highlighting the unique value of spatial inversion products in filling the "spatial gap" of traditional monitoring networks.

4.2. Limitations and Future Works
While our results demonstrate the clear superiority of the HIOBF framework, a critical discussion of its underlying assumptions and operational limitations is warranted. One central challenge relates to the spatial representation of the inversion data. The assignment of a single pixel value to represent the average condition at a SWAT sub-basin outlet is a necessary simplification to reconcile scale mismatches. This process, however, can introduce representation errors, particularly in heterogeneous sub-basins where the selected pixel may not fully capture the integrated hydrological response. Furthermore, although the inversion data set shows strong aggregate performance, it is not free from localized biases. This was evident in the Ruhu sub-basin, where the PIBF scenario degraded performance relative to the POBF baseline. This phenomenon may suggest that for certain sub-basins (e.g., Ruhu), whose geomorphological characteristics or pollution source profiles might differ significantly from those used to train the spatial extrapolation model, the inferred Z-U relationship could introduce bias.

Our framework, like many Bayesian applications, relies on a Gaussian error assumption. While the autoregressive correction effectively addresses temporal dependence, other forms of non-Gaussian behavior or more complex heteroscedastic relationships may still persist (Ammann et al., 2019; Schoups & Vrugt, 2010; Samadi et al., 2017). Addressing these more intricate error structures remains an important avenue for future methodological refinement.
Our sequential calibration strategy--where hydrological parameters were fixed deterministically prior to TN fusion--theoretically underestimates the total predictive uncertainty by neglecting the propagation of hydrological simulation errors. Moreover, the incorrect assumption of deterministic hydrological parameters may introduce additional errors in the estimated water quality parameters as the inference process forces nutrient parameters to compensate for biases in the underlying hydrological drivers (Guzman et al., 2015; Zhang & Shao, 2018). Nevertheless, given the substantial performance margin of HIOBF over the traditional in situ observation-based baseline (as shown in Figures 7 and 8), our core conclusion remains robust: integrating spatial inversion TN data via the HIOBF framework significantly improves SWAT simulation accuracy in ungauged sub-basins. Future studies could systematically estimate the total uncertainty via a comprehensive uncertainty estimation framework that incorporates uncertainties from model structure, parameter estimation, and data observation errors from all sources (in situ and spatial inversed) (Moges et al., 2021; Moreno-Rodenas et al., 2019).
The reliability of the HIOBF framework hinges on the performance of the spatial extrapolation model. The high predictive accuracy (R2 > 0.8) achieved during internal validation provides strong support for its application in the DJB. This success is likely attributable to the rich set of geomorphological predictors that effectively capture the drivers of spatial heterogeneity. Nonetheless, the extrapolation model remains region-specific; applying the same coefficients to vastly different hydro-climatic regions would require re-training. Thus, while the HIOBF methodology is transferable, the specific model parameters are not universal. Future research could explore advanced machine learning techniques, such as Graph Neural Networks (Wan et al., 2025), to learn more generalizable spatial relationships, further enhancing the model's robustness and transferability in basins with limited observational data.
Our model performance (Figures 7 and 8 and Table 2) can be further improved by incorporating deep learning (DL) algorithms with the SWAT model. This can be done by using DL to estimate SWAT modeling residuals (Yifru et al., 2024), or using SWAT outputs as inputs of DL for TN prediction at ungauged sub-basins (Huan et al., 2025; Jeong et al., 2026). The integration of DL and HIOBF-calibrated SWAT is complementary, as the improved SWAT parameterization based on HIOBF allows more accurate and transferable physical process description, that is, usually absent in DL, while DL can significantly improve the final TN prediction accuracy via its data fitting capacity. A more integrated approach could treat DL-derived residuals as informative signals to dynamically update SWAT parameters via data assimilation algorithms like the Ensemble Kalman Filter (Liu et al., 2025). However, this results in dynamically evolving parameters, which may limit the model's reliability for long-term planning applications such as BMP optimization. For a robust, theory-guided integration, a Physics- Informed Neural Network framework could be applied. By using hydrological and water quality governing equations to constrain the DL model, PINNs offer a promising avenue for synthesizing DL, process-based models, and spatial inversion data with high precision (Lawal et al., 2022; Zhao et al., 2024), representing a valuable direction for future research.
Finally, our study aligns with ongoing efforts to integrate large-volume, alternative data sources--such as spatial inversion products, low-cost sensor networks, social media mining, and crowdsourced data--with conventional high-quality, low-volume in situ measurements in hydrological and water quality modeling (Han et al., 2023; Songchon et al., 2023; Wongchuig et al., 2024). Similar to spatial inversion data, the error structures of these alternative sources are often complex, heterogeneous, and spatially non-stationary due to variations in sensor accuracy, maintenance protocols, and operator experience (Jayaraman et al., 2024; Ribeiro et al., 2022; Wu et al., 2025). Although the importance of observation error in such data is widely recognized, many existing approaches still rely on oversimplified, homogeneous error assumptions (Cho et al., 2020; Rasmussen et al., 2016). The HIOBF framework addresses this limitation by providing a generalizable methodology to characterize spatially variable error structures, thereby advancing robust multi-source data integration for hydrological modeling in future studies.

5. Conclusions
This study addressed the persistent challenge of accurately simulating watershed-scale total nitrogen (TN) dynamics in data-scarce regions. By developing and rigorously testing a novel HIOBF framework, we demonstrated a powerful and transferable methodology for systematically integrating high-frequency spatial inversion data with traditional in situ measurements. The core innovation of our work lies not only in the combination of diverse data sets, but in the creation of a statistically robust framework that explicitly characterizes the complex error structures inherent in spatial data streams. This breakthrough enables their coherent and formal assimilation within a unified Bayesian process, overcoming the theoretical barriers that have previously limited the use of inversion data in distributed modeling.
The principal conclusions of this work are as follows:
1. A primary methodological contribution of this research is the development of a geography-driven spatial extrapolation model, which adequately addresses the fundamental problem of defining Bayesian likelihood functions in ungauged regions. By inferring the complex error relationship between in situ measurements and spatial inversion data based on catchment characteristics, it allows for the construction of a robust error model when ground truth is absent.
2. The rigorous independent validation results unequivocally demonstrate that the HIOBF framework consistently outperforms both the observation-only (POBF) and inversion-only (PIBF) baselines. By synergistically combining the spatial coverage of inversion data with the temporal precision of in situ observations, HIOBF yields a more constrained and robust posterior parameter set. This leads to a significant enhancement in predictive skill, increasing the NSE by an average of 55% relative to the traditional POBF baseline across independent validation sites.
3. The value of data fusion lies in synergy, not just volume. The Supporting Information S1 analysis on data availability revealed that the benefits of the HIOBF framework are evident even under severely data-limited conditions. The significant performance lift achieved by fusing just one or two sub-basins of each data type indicates that the primary value is derived from the complementarity of information types, rather than the sheer quantity of data assimilated. This suggests that for practical watershed management, a cost-effective strategy may involve deploying a sparse but strategically placed network of ground sensors augmented by spatially continuous inversion data.
In summary, this research provides compelling evidence that the structured integration of high-frequency spatial inversion data within a formal Bayesian framework offers a powerful and scalable solution for advancing water quality modeling. The HIOBF approach enhances methodological rigor and practical utility by reducing parameter uncertainty, increasing predictive accuracy, and producing more reliable uncertainty estimates. This paradigm provides a promising pathway for data-informed watershed management and can be readily extended to other water quality constituents in diverse hydrological settings.
